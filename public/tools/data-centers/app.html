How this works

Demand. 2025 demand of 4,532 TWh (Ember) is split by end use. Data centers are about 246 TWh: LBNL's 2018 and 2023 estimates (76 and 176 TWh), carried forward at their 18%/yr pace. LBNL's 2028 range of 325 to 580 TWh implies 13 to 27%/yr. Transportation is about 28 TWh (EV charging plus rail), growing about 20%/yr. Industry is about 27% of demand, and buildings (homes and non-data-center commercial) are the rest. Together, buildings and industry grew about 0.7%/yr over 2019 to 2025. Data-center and transport growth slow after the first timing year and reach the buildings and industry rate by the second. The top-line slider adds the same amount to every end-use rate. It reads as the average growth of total demand from 2025 to 2035.

Clean generation. Solar and wind fleets grow by each year's additions, with each source on its own rate. In 2023 to 2025, the U.S. added an average of 33 GW/yr of solar and 6 GW/yr of wind. Over 2016 to 2025, solar additions grew 19%/yr and wind additions fell 3%/yr. Nuclear changes by a net GW/yr. Hydro, bioenergy, other renewables and imports share one growth rate (−0.7%/yr over 2015 to 2025). 2025 capacity factors turn GW into TWh. The top-line slider adds the same amount to the solar, wind and other-clean rates. It reads as the average growth of clean generation from 2025 to 2035.

Fossil. Coal and gas supply whatever demand is left. When that is less than in 2025, both scale down together. When it is more, gas covers the increase: first the existing fleet runs harder, then new plants are built. Coal retires 9 GW/yr (2020 to 2025 average) and gas 3 GW/yr.

Emissions and targets. Generation times 2025 lifecycle intensity by source (Ember), against Ember's 2005 total of 2,439 Mt. The U.S. economy-wide NDC targets, 50 to 52% below 2005 by 2030 and 61 to 66% by 2035, are applied to the power sector one for one. That reading is lenient, because most pathways cut power faster than the rest of the economy. The 100% clean electricity goal for 2035 and net zero by 2050 are shown as zero. The U.S. has since withdrawn from the Paris Agreement, so these are benchmarks.

Limits. This is an annual energy balance with no hourly matching or storage, so it overstates how much fossil power solar displaces once solar's share is large. Coal and gas output does not respond to fuel prices.

Data: Ember yearly electricity data (generation, capacity and emissions to 2025); Shehabi et al. (LBNL, 2024), United States Data Center Energy Usage Report.
